## Why uncompressed images hurt your website

Images account for roughly half of every page's weight. A single 5 MB phone photo on a landing page can push load times past the 2.5 second Largest Contentful Paint threshold that Google uses for ranking. On mobile networks, that means real people bouncing before your content appears.

Compression fixes this immediately. The browser downloads fewer bytes, the page paints faster, and Core Web Vitals improve — often without touching a single line of code.

## Lossy vs lossless: what you are actually choosing

**Lossy compression** (JPG, WebP, AVIF) removes information that is statistically least visible — fine texture, subtle gradients, tiny details in noise. Pushed too far, you get blocky artifacts around edges. Kept at a sane quality level, nobody can tell.

**Lossless compression** (PNG, lossless WebP) stores every pixel exactly. Files stay bigger, but screenshots, logos and text-heavy graphics remain pixel-perfect.

The practical rule:

- Photos → lossy, quality 80
- Screenshots with text → lossy WebP at quality 90, or PNG if the file must be pixel-identical
- Logos and icons with transparency → PNG or lossless WebP

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Compressing a compressed file twice.** Each lossy pass discards more data. Always keep originals and export once.
- **Using quality 100.** Maximum quality roughly doubles file size for almost no visible gain.
- **Saving photos as PNG.** PNG is designed for graphics, not photographs — a photo PNG can be 10× larger than the same image as JPG.
- **Ignoring dimensions.** A 6000 px image displayed at 800 px wastes 90% of its bytes. [Resize first](/resize-image), then compress.

## FAQ

### Does compressing an image reduce quality?

Slightly, yes — lossy formats discard data. At quality 80 the change is virtually invisible on normal screens. Keep your original file if you plan to edit or print the image later.
