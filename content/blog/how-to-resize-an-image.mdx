Resizing an image without blurring comes down to how the pixels are resampled: downscale in repeated halving steps instead of one giant jump, and keep the aspect ratio locked so nothing stretches. This is exactly what the [free resizer on this site](/resize-image) does automatically — drop a photo, set a width, and download a sharp result in seconds.

## Why resized images usually look soft

When you shrink a 4000 px photo to 800 px in a single operation, the algorithm has to average 25 source pixels into every output pixel. Details that span several pixels — hair, text, foliage, fabric weave — collapse into grey mush. Operating systems and naive tools do exactly this one-shot resize, which is why most resized images look blurry.

Progressive downscaling avoids the problem: resize to half, then half again, and so on. At each step the algorithm only has to merge a 2×2 neighborhood, preserving contrast and edges far better. The final image looks noticeably crisper at the same dimensions.

## What dimensions should you actually use?

These defaults work well across most websites:

- **Full-width hero image:** 1920 px wide (1600 px if you want leaner pages)
- **Blog content image:** 800–1200 px wide
- **Thumbnail:** 300–400 px wide
- **Instagram/web export:** 1080 px on the longest side
- **Email header:** 600 px wide

Export at 2× (for example, a 1200 px image displayed at 600 px) if you need crispness on retina screens and can spare the extra bytes.

## Resize first, compress second — in one pass

Dimensions dominate file size. A 6000 px photo cannot be saved by quality tweaks alone; downscaling to 1600 px cuts the pixel count by 96%, and compression finishes the job. The two operations combined routinely take a 8 MB camera photo under 200 KB — a 97% reduction with no visible damage.

## Common resizing mistakes

- **Stretching to fit.** Forcing both width and height independently distorts the image. Always scale proportionally.
- **Upscaling small images.** Enlarging beyond the original dimensions invents pixels and looks soft. Downscale only.
- **Repeatedly re-saving at new sizes.** Every pass degrades a lossy file. Resize once from the original.
- **Ignoring file format.** A resized photo should be WebP or JPG, not PNG — see the [format comparison](/blog/jpg-vs-png-vs-webp-vs-avif).

## FAQ

### How do I resize an image without losing quality?

Use step-wise downscaling (halving repeatedly) rather than one-shot scaling, and export from your highest-quality original exactly once. The [resizer on this site](/resize-image) does this automatically.

### Does resizing change the aspect ratio?

Not with proportional modes like "set width" or "max longest side" — the second dimension is calculated automatically so the image is never stretched.

### What is the best size for a website image?

1600 px on the longest side for hero images and 800–1200 px for content images strike the best balance between sharpness and page weight on modern screens.
