The short answer: use **JPG for photos**, **PNG for graphics that need transparency or pixel-perfect text**, **WebP as the default for modern websites**, and **AVIF when you want the smallest possible files**. WebP and AVIF both support transparency and beat JPG on size at equal quality, and every format here works with the [free converters on this site](/formats).

## JPG: the reliable workhorse

JPG has been the standard for photographs since the 1990s, and its strength is still real: excellent compression for continuous-tone images and universal compatibility — literally every app, camera and website accepts it.

The drawbacks: no transparency, quality degrades a little each time you re-save, and modern formats simply beat it on efficiency. Use JPG when you need maximum compatibility or are dealing with legacy systems.

## PNG: lossless precision

PNG stores every pixel exactly, supports full alpha-channel transparency, and renders sharp text and hard edges perfectly. That makes it the right choice for screenshots, logos, icons and diagrams.

The cost is size. Photographic content saved as PNG is typically 5–10× larger than the same image as JPG. If your PNG contains a photo, [convert it to WebP or JPG](/png-to-webp) and watch the file shrink dramatically.

## WebP: the modern default

Google introduced WebP in 2010 and every mainstream browser has supported it for years. It uses prediction models to beat JPG by 25–50% at the same visual quality while still supporting transparency and animation.

For blogs, stores, and landing pages, WebP should be your default export format. Serve it with a JPG fallback via the HTML `<picture>` element if you still support ancient software.

## AVIF: the smallest files

AVIF comes from the AV1 video codec and typically lands 20–40% below WebP at comparable quality — especially at lower quality settings where older codecs fall apart. It also handles high dynamic range and 10-bit color.

The trade-off is encoding speed: AVIF takes noticeably longer to produce, which matters little for one-off web assets and a lot for bulk video-frame work. Modern browsers support it, but you should still ship fallbacks.

## Head-to-head comparison

| Format | Compression | Transparency | Best for | Relative size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| JPG | Lossy | No | Photos, legacy support | Medium |
| PNG | Lossless | Yes | Screenshots, logos | Large |
| WebP | Lossy + lossless | Yes | Everyday web images | Small |
| AVIF | Lossy + lossless | Yes | Smallest web assets | Smallest |

## A one-line decision rule

Ask what the image contains:

1. **Photo** → WebP (fallback JPG)
2. **Screenshot or logo with transparency** → PNG for editing, WebP/AVIF for publishing
3. **Must work in a decades-old app** → JPG
4. **Every kilobyte counts** → AVIF

## FAQ

### Which format has the best quality-to-size ratio?

AVIF currently leads, with WebP close behind. Both beat JPG and massively beat PNG for photographic content.

### Is WebP safe to use in 2026?

Yes — all modern browsers support it, and Google, Wikipedia and major CDNs serve WebP by default. Provide a JPG or PNG fallback only if you must support very old clients.

### Should screenshots be PNG or WebP?

PNG is the safe editing format; WebP at quality 90+ is the better publishing format because it keeps text crisp at a fraction of the size. Test the output on your own screenshots before committing.
